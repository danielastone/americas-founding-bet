# America’s Founding Bet

A source-driven test of how Atlantic commodity trade became federal fiscal capacity between 1789 and the Louisiana Purchase.

## Thesis under test

Between 1789 and 1803, the United States converted customs receipts generated by Atlantic commodity trade into dependable debt service and marketable public obligations. Port customhouses, merchant credit, the First Bank, and European intermediaries connected physical cargoes to federal finance. The Louisiana Purchase is the endpoint and stress test of that capacity: the government used public obligations and transatlantic finance to acquire a claim of sovereignty over the Mississippi–New Orleans corridor.

This thesis does **not** assume that the founding financial system caused the Purchase, created Mississippi commerce, or distributed gains equally. Foreign markets, imperial warfare, merchant concentration, Indigenous dispossession, and commodities produced through enslaved labor were conditions of the system, not side notes.

## Scope

- **Core period:** 1789–1803
- **Endpoint:** the Louisiana Purchase as a financing and political-risk event
- **1804 exception:** only records completing the transfer, claims settlement, or financing authorized in 1803
- **Unit of proof:** linked transactions and reconciled accounts, not thematic narrative
- **Out of scope:** later territorial expansion, a general history of capitalism, comprehensive consumer welfare, and standalone histories of infrastructure or diplomacy

## Empirical spine

1. A cargo enters or leaves a selected port.
2. A merchant finances the cargo, duty, insurance, inventory, or re-export.
3. A customhouse assesses an obligation and realizes—or fails to realize—cash.
4. Treasury accounts place that cash within federal revenue and debt service.
5. Debt performance affects the price and marketability of federal obligations.
6. The First Bank and European intermediaries transmit or extend financing capacity.
7. The 1803 purchase obligations reveal how that capacity worked at the endpoint.

A source belongs in the core only if it supplies evidence for one of these links.

## Minimum viable project

The first publishable version requires five deliverables:

1. **Federal fiscal table, 1789–1803:** customs cash, debt service, Bank flows, foreign financing, and Louisiana obligations on reconciled accounting bases.
2. **Tariff and customs chronology:** statutory rates, credit terms, bonding, drawbacks, collection rules, and observed implementation through 1803.
3. **Comparable port evidence:** one defensible Atlantic port pair first; expansion only after a coverage audit.
4. **Merchant transaction:** at least one end-to-end cargo–voyage–customs–credit reconstruction.
5. **Louisiana reconciliation:** authorization, claims convention, securities, cash proceeds, intermediaries, fees, exchange, and the narrow 1804 execution tail.

No new thematic layer enters the core until these exist.

## Analytical hierarchy

| Role | Material |
|---|---|
| Core evidence | Federal accounts, customs records, tariff law, securities, purchase finance, linked merchant and cargo records |
| Mechanism tests | First Bank operations, customs bonding, drawbacks, re-export finance, port credit |
| External conditions | Haiti/Saint-Domingue, European war and credit, Pinckney’s Treaty, foreign commodity demand |
| Distributional context | Enslaved production, regional and merchant concentration, selected household-price evidence |
| Supporting infrastructure | Revenue cutters, lighthouses, storage, navigation |
| Deferred | Broad global datasets, comprehensive welfare estimates, full BIBFRAME implementation, large econometric models, publication expansion |

## Research rules

- Separate duties assessed, bonds issued, and cash collected.
- Separate imports, domestic exports, foreign re-exports, and coastwise trade.
- Separate debt face value, market value, cash proceeds, interest, and redemption.
- Treat statutes and institutional designs as authority, not proof of implementation.
- Apply the audit evidence hierarchy before advancing a claim.
- Do not infer production origin from port exports or profit from trade volume.
- Record contradictory and missing evidence.
- Require an explicit counterfactual before making a causal claim.
- Keep New Orleans outside a mechanically pooled U.S.-port panel before transfer.
- Use BIBFRAME for bibliographic description only; keep archival hierarchy, observations, entities, and claims in their own tables.
