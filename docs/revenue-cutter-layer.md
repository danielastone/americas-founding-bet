# Revenue Cutter layer

## Core question

Did the early Revenue Marine turn customs law into credible enforcement at sea, and at what cost?

Authorization is not performance. The layer must trace appropriations, vessel acquisition, staffing, instructions, patrols, boardings, manifest checks, seizures, prosecutions, operating costs, and the relationship between cutters and customs collectors.

## Evidence sequence

1. Statutory authority and geographic jurisdiction.
2. Cutter authorization, construction, purchase, and commissioning.
3. Treasury and collector instructions.
4. Personnel, provisions, pay, maintenance, and readiness.
5. Patrols, boardings, searches, seizures, assistance, and other actions.
6. Court or customs disposition of enforcement actions.
7. Revenue protected or recovered, with uncertainty stated.

## First controlled test

Reconstruct the first ten authorized cutters from 1790 through 1793. For each, establish acquisition date, station, collector relationship, surviving instructions, documented patrol activity, and cost. Do not fill missing logs with later institutional histories.

## Constraints

- Early log survival is incomplete; absence of a log is not absence of activity.
- Seizures measure detected cases, enforcement priorities, and record survival—not total smuggling.
- Gross customs revenue cannot be attributed to cutter activity without a defensible counterfactual.
- Cutter officers were customs officers, but cutters and land-based customhouses had different functions.
- Later Coast Guard missions must not be projected backward onto the 1790s service.
