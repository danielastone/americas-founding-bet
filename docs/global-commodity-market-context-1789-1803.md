# Global commodity-market context, 1789–1803

## Purpose

This layer supplies external controls and comparative context for the American finance–commodity system. It does not expand the project's core geography. The question is:

> Which changes in American revenue, credit, prices, shipping, and commodity flows reflected domestic institutional capacity, and which reflected global demand, supply, war, empire, harvest, finance, or shipping conditions?

## Correct model of the market

There was no single integrated global commodity market in the modern sense. The late eighteenth-century trading system consisted of connected but segmented circuits:

- Atlantic plantation and provisioning trades
- European metropolitan and colonial markets
- Baltic grain, timber, hemp, iron, and naval-stores routes
- Mediterranean grain and regional exchange
- Indian Ocean and Asian tea, textiles, spices, and silver settlements
- North American coastal, transatlantic, Caribbean, and interior routes

Prices could influence one another without converging quickly. Imperial preferences, chartered-company monopolies, navigation laws, war, blockades, port rules, currencies, product grades, and slow information created persistent wedges.

The United States entered this system as a politically independent but financially and commercially dependent participant. Independence removed some imperial restrictions while also removing guaranteed access and protection.

## Structural conditions

### 1. Europe remained the major external demand and finance center

Britain, France, the Netherlands, Spain, and their commercial cities supplied demand, shipping services, insurance, bills of exchange, and investment capital. London and Amsterdam mattered to the United States not only as destinations but as settlement and credit centers.

American export performance therefore depended on:

- European harvests and consumption
- Manufacturing demand
- War provisioning
- Exchange rates and specie availability
- Merchant-credit conditions
- Freight and marine-insurance costs
- Access under imperial and wartime rules

A rise in American exports can reflect foreign scarcity or wartime intermediation rather than improved American productivity.

### 2. Plantation commodities joined mass consumption to coerced production

Sugar, molasses, coffee, tobacco, rice, and increasingly cotton were embedded in plantation systems dependent on enslaved labor. European consumer demand, merchant credit, shipping, insurance, and state revenue were inseparable from coercion.

The project must not model slavery as a generic “input.” It should identify:

- The producing jurisdiction and labor regime
- Enslaved population or labor evidence where available
- Plantation, factor, merchant, insurer, creditor, and consignee relationships
- Who received revenue, interest, commissions, profit, and loss
- How revolt, resistance, mortality, and emancipation altered supply

### 3. War changed routes, risks, and legal categories

The wars beginning in 1792–1793 disrupted established imperial shipping and created space for neutral American carriers. The same system generated seizure, condemnation, embargo, insurance loss, delayed remittance, and diplomatic conflict.

Neutrality was a contingent commercial position, not a permanent comparative advantage. Measured American shipping growth may combine:

- Genuine expansion in capacity
- Reflagging or rerouting
- Foreign goods carried by American vessels
- Re-export through American ports
- Wartime freight premiums
- Trade displaced from belligerent carriers
- Greater exposure to capture

The Prize Papers are unusually valuable because captures preserved cargo papers and correspondence. They are also a selected sample of failed or contested voyages.

### 4. Information and credit were part of commodity prices

A merchant did not face one observable world price. The relevant decision price depended on dated information and financing:

Sale proceeds  
minus purchase cost  
minus inland and ocean freight  
minus insurance  
minus duties retained  
minus commissions and storage  
minus exchange and remittance cost  
minus expected seizure, spoilage, and default losses  
minus the cost of capital while funds were tied up.

Newspapers, price currents, correspondence, ship arrivals, bills of exchange, brokers, auctions, and insurers formed the information system. Delays and asymmetric information could create both arbitrage profits and large losses.

### 5. Physical characteristics shaped market geography

| Commodity group | Market structure | Main physical constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar, coffee, molasses | Plantation supply, refining, re-export, mass consumption | Spoilage, grades, casks, colonial access |
| Tobacco | Consignment, inspection, European demand, established credit chains | Quality variation, bulky storage, inspection |
| Flour and grain | Harvest-sensitive provisioning and urban demand | Low value-to-weight ratio, spoilage, milling and transport |
| Cotton | Expanding British industrial demand and plantation production | Quality, cleaning, baling, inland transport |
| Rice and indigo | Concentrated regional production and Atlantic export | Processing, grade, climate, labor regime |
| Tea and Asian textiles | Long-distance chartered-company and private trade | Voyage duration, silver or exchange settlement, monopoly restrictions |
| Timber, hemp, iron, naval stores | Baltic and North American strategic supply | Bulk, freight, state procurement, wartime demand |

A common value index can conceal entirely different financing durations, risks, units, and routes.

## Commodity-specific context for the American project

### Sugar, coffee, and molasses

Saint-Domingue was central to late eighteenth-century Atlantic plantation trade. Revolution and war disrupted production and imperial routes, affecting prices, re-export opportunities, refugee and merchant networks, seizures, and diplomatic policy.

For the project, these are primarily foreign goods and Caribbean-system indicators. They should not be counted as American production when imported and re-exported. Their value lies in testing customs revenue, merchant working capital, drawbacks, shipping, and shock transmission.

### Tobacco

Tobacco linked Chesapeake production to European demand through inspection systems, factors, consignments, bills, and long-standing British credit relationships. State inspection and warehouse records can help connect production jurisdictions to Baltimore or other ports.

The analytical risk is confusing port clearance with state of origin and gross shipment value with planter or merchant income.

### Flour, grain, and provisions

Foodstuffs connected Mid-Atlantic and western hinterlands to Atlantic, Caribbean, and urban provisioning markets. Weather and harvest shocks could dominate short-run prices. Milling quality, barrels, roads, rivers, and freight determined the economically relevant market radius.

This is the most direct commodity family for testing the Mississippi-access argument. The Louisiana Purchase did not create foreign demand; it altered control and expected security of an export route.

### Cotton

Cotton was becoming more important before 1803 because of British industrial demand and expanding slave-based production. It should appear as a rising commodity, not be projected backward as already dominant.

The research must separate technological, demand, land-expansion, labor-coercion, credit, and port-access mechanisms.

### Rice and indigo

These commodities connect South Carolina's state fiscal and plantation system to Charleston's export economy. They provide a useful comparison with the more diversified Mid-Atlantic ports, but only if consistent quantities, grades, and origin evidence survive.

### Asian imports

Tea, textiles, and other Asian goods affected customs revenue, consumption, specie or exchange settlement, and long-distance shipping. They are part of the federal import base even if they are not core commodity corridors.

Adding a full China-trade narrative would exceed scope unless the evidence shows that Asian imports materially change the customs-revenue or merchant-finance conclusions.

## Global shock chronology

| Period | External condition | American mechanism to test |
|---|---|---|
| 1789–1791 | Post-independence market adjustment; new federal tariff and funding regime | Import response, customs cash, merchant adaptation |
| 1792 | Securities and credit disturbance | Bank, Treasury, merchant liquidity |
| 1791–1794 | Haitian Revolution and escalating Caribbean disruption | Sugar/coffee supply, refugee networks, re-export, seizures |
| 1793 onward | European war and neutral-carrier opportunity | Freight, insurance, American tonnage, re-export, capture |
| 1794–1796 | Embargo, Jay and Pinckney diplomacy, contested neutral rights | Route access, western expectations, merchant interruption |
| 1797–1800 | British monetary restriction and Atlantic credit stress | Bills, exchange, defaults, remittance, federal debt finance |
| 1801–1803 | Renewed European war and Mississippi access crisis | Commodity route valuation and Purchase finance |

The chronology identifies candidate shocks. It does not prove causal effects.

## External datasets and archives

### TOFLIT18 — AFB-S073

French external trade from 1716–1821 with products and partners. This is the strongest direct external benchmark for French trade, Saint-Domingue disruption, wartime breaks, and changing commodity composition.

Use it to test whether American re-export or commodity movements mirror changes in French trade. Do not assume French classifications or values match U.S. customs definitions.

### RICardo — AFB-S074

Bilateral trade data beginning in 1787. Early coverage is sparse. Use it to locate original trade publications and build selected bilateral comparisons, not a complete 1789–1803 world panel.

### Sound Toll Registers Online — AFB-S075

Voyage and cargo records for traffic through the Danish Sound. Use for Baltic grain, naval stores, coffee redistribution, and shipping conditions affecting European markets. It is not an Atlantic trade census.

### Navigocorpus — AFB-S076

Heterogeneous shipping observations from the sixteenth through nineteenth centuries. Use for vessel, port, route, and cargo leads and to cross-check movements. Preserve each underlying source.

### Allen–Unger prices — AFB-S077

Long-run commodity price series. Use for external grain and other price controls after reconciling grade, unit, currency, market convention, and frequency.

### Lloyd's Register — AFB-S078

Use to identify and validate vessels, ownership, build, tonnage, and classification. Registration is not evidence of a specific voyage or cargo.

### British Prize Papers — AFB-S079

Use for captured ships, manifests, accounts, bills, letters, insurance, and adjudication from the wartime period. Because capture determined preservation, these records cannot estimate ordinary voyage success without a comparison group.

### SlaveVoyages — AFB-S023–S024

Use to quantify and contextualize coerced Atlantic movement and to link voyages to underlying archival citations. Do not merge enslaved-person movements into an ordinary commodity table.

## Empirical use

For port, commodity, or merchant outcomes, candidate controls include:

- External commodity price
- Freight rate
- Marine insurance premium
- Sterling–dollar and guilder–dollar exchange conditions
- European or Caribbean harvest shock
- War and neutrality regime
- Imperial trade restriction
- Capture intensity
- Foreign credit shock
- Destination demand
- Commodity-specific seasonality

Controls should not be added mechanically. Each must match the route, commodity, and decision date faced by the merchant.

## Tests that matter

1. Do American export quantities rise after controlling for foreign prices and war demand?
2. Do re-exports move inversely with disrupted French or Caribbean direct trade?
3. Do customs receipts reflect higher import volume, higher prices, tariff changes, or delayed bond collection?
4. Does American shipping growth persist outside wartime neutral-carrier windows?
5. Do port outcomes differ because of financial depth after controlling for commodity mix and geography?
6. Did western access change expected route costs before measurable post-Purchase volumes appeared?
7. Who captured wartime margins, and who bore seizure, insurance, and default losses?

## Boundaries

The global context layer is complete when it supplies defensible external variables for the U.S. tests. It does not require:

- A comprehensive world trade reconstruction
- Full histories of British, French, Dutch, Spanish, or Asian empires
- A global general-equilibrium model
- Commodity narratives lacking a U.S. fiscal, port, state, or merchant link
- Post-1803 global trends except for validation

If an external dataset cannot change an American estimate, identify a shock, or validate a route, it does not belong in the core repository.
