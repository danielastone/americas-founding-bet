# Top-down research framework, 1789–1803

## Decision rule

A proposed source, dataset, or narrative layer belongs in the core project only if it answers all three questions:

1. What finance mechanism or physical commodity flow does it measure?
2. At which level—federal, state, city/port, or merchant—does it operate?
3. How does it affect the causal chain ending with the Louisiana Purchase as financed access to the Mississippi–New Orleans corridor?

Material failing this test is background, comparative evidence, or out of scope.

## Primary estimand

The project cannot credibly estimate one universal “effect of the founding.” Its central empirical target is narrower:

> Changes in federal fiscal capacity and commercial intermediation between 1789 and 1803, measured through revenue realization, financing costs, payment and credit networks, and the volume, composition, routing, and disruption of physical commodity trade.

The endpoint claim is not that the Louisiana Purchase caused immediate growth. It is that the United States assembled the financial and diplomatic capacity to purchase an option on future western market access.

## Causal structure

Physical imports and shipping → customs assessment and collection → federal debt service and credit → bank and merchant finance → commodity exports and re-exports. Federal credit also supports diplomacy and purchase finance → Mississippi–New Orleans access → expanded export options.

This is a feedback system, not a one-way success story. War, default, seizure, exclusion, slavery, and regional concentration can weaken or redistribute every link.

## Observation spine

The project should be built around linked observations rather than thematic essays.

### Entities

- Federal agency, account, security, statute, treaty
- State and state-chartered bank
- City, port, and customs district
- Merchant, firm, partner, director, shareholder, surety, insurer, and correspondent
- Vessel and voyage
- Commodity and cargo line
- Financial instrument
- Archival item and source series

### Events

- Import entry and duty assessment
- Customs bond execution, maturity, payment, or default
- Export or re-export
- Drawback claim, allowance, and payment
- Bill, discount, insurance policy, remittance, and settlement
- Federal security subscription, issue, transfer, interest, and redemption
- Treasury deposit, transfer, and disbursement
- Bank branch opening and transaction
- Seizure, embargo, market shock, treaty, and territorial payment

## Level-by-level research questions

### Federal

- How dependent was the federal government on customs revenue?
- How much assessed revenue became usable cash, and when?
- What did debt funding and European placement cost in net proceeds and debt service?
- How did the First Bank change Treasury collections, transfers, balances, and borrowing?
- Could the United States finance the Louisiana Purchase without destabilizing existing obligations?

### State

- How did assumption alter state debt burdens and federal–state fiscal relationships?
- Which state banking and tax systems complemented or competed with federal institutions?
- Which states produced exportable staples, and through which ports did those commodities move?
- Did state boundaries correspond to commodity flows, or did commercial hinterlands cross them?

The state level should remain subordinate to measurable fiscal and commodity links. A general history of state policy would recreate the scope problem.

### City and port

- Which ports generated federal revenue?
- Which ports supplied banking, insurance, exchange, storage, and customs services?
- How concentrated were commodities, destinations, merchants, and shocks?
- Did a port's financial depth predict cargo throughput or resilience after controlling for geography and commodity mix?

Cities are analytical nodes, not standalone biographies.

### Merchant

- How was a cargo financed from purchase through sale or re-export?
- How did customs bonds and drawbacks affect working capital?
- Which merchants accessed Bank discounts, surety networks, insurance, and foreign correspondents?
- How did default, seizure, price change, and payment delay distribute losses?

Merchant case studies must be sampled to test mechanisms, not selected only because records are vivid.

## Commodity design

Use a deliberately small basket with different economic roles:

| Role | Candidate commodities | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign import and re-export | Sugar, coffee, molasses | Customs, Caribbean disruption, drawback, re-export |
| Established domestic staple | Tobacco | State production, Atlantic export, merchant finance |
| Food and western access | Flour, wheat, corn, pork where records support them | Port hinterlands and Mississippi access |
| Rapid-growth staple | Cotton | Southern expansion, foreign demand, slavery, changing export composition |

Commodity inclusion depends on consistent quantities, units, origin, destination, and price evidence. A commodity does not enter the core simply because it is historically important.

## Geographic design

### Core ports

- New York
- Philadelphia
- Baltimore
- Charleston
- New Orleans at the 1803 endpoint

### Supporting geographies

- State hinterlands feeding the core ports
- Saint-Domingue/Haiti and selected Caribbean ports
- London and Amsterdam financial markets
- Mississippi and Ohio valley export routes
- Spanish-controlled New Orleans before transfer

A balanced five-port panel may be impossible because surviving series differ. The project should publish an unbalanced evidence map rather than manufacture comparability.

## Time design

| Period | Analytical role |
|---|---|
| Before 1789 | Institutional and fiscal baseline only |
| 1789–1791 | Customs, funding, assumption, and Bank design |
| 1792–1796 | Implementation, panic, European war, Haiti, Pinckney |
| 1797–1800 | Credit stress, enforcement, re-export, political transition |
| 1801–1803 | Mississippi access crisis and Louisiana Purchase finance |
| After 1803 | Validation or consequence only; excluded from main estimates |

## Claim hierarchy

### Tier 1: accounting identities

Examples: customs cash received; debt principal; interest paid; cargo quantities; bond maturity; drawback paid.

### Tier 2: reconstructed mechanisms

Examples: working-capital interval; merchant exposure; Treasury transfer time; re-export margin; port concentration.

### Tier 3: causal claims

Examples: Bank access reduced financing cost; Pinckney increased western export options; customs credit increased re-export; the Purchase was fiscally feasible because of prior credit formation.

Tier 3 claims require explicit counterfactuals and robustness tests. They cannot be promoted from descriptive chronology.

## Minimum viable project

The project is publishable before every layer is complete if it produces:

1. A reconciled federal fiscal series for customs, debt service, Bank flows, foreign loans, and Purchase obligations.
2. A documented port-level trade panel separating imports, domestic exports, and re-exports.
3. A linked merchant sample showing cargo, voyage, customs obligation, and finance.
4. Two commodity corridors: one Caribbean re-export chain and one western/domestic export chain.
5. A Louisiana Purchase synthesis that distinguishes financing, legal access, and realized trade.

Everything else is optional until those five outputs exist.

## Priority order

1. Freeze definitions, period, geography, commodities, and accounting bases.
2. Build the federal fiscal spine.
3. Inventory comparable port and customs series.
4. Build one end-to-end merchant transaction case.
5. Expand to the two commodity corridors.
6. Test shock and distributional hypotheses.
7. Write the Louisiana Purchase synthesis.
8. Add comparative or contextual material only after the core survives validation.

## Stop conditions

Do not add a new layer when:

- It mainly begins after 1803.
- It cannot be linked to finance or a physical commodity flow.
- It lacks a defined observation and source path.
- It duplicates an existing mechanism under a new theme.
- It adds narrative breadth without improving identification.
- It requires a new national history before the current empirical spine exists.
